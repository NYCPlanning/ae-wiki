
# Application Engineering Goal Setting Overview

In collaboration with your manager, you may lay out specific, measurable, and reachable goals on a 6 or 12 month repeating cadence, with a brief check-ins halfway through the cycle. Participation in this process is optional but highly encouraged.

## Why do goal setting?

When done well, this goal setting process serves a few purposes. First and foremost, it provides a framework for you to communicate your professional development goals to your manager so that those goals can be factored into work planning. It also creates a positive feedback loop as you track yourself getting closer to your goals. In our day-to-day, it's easy to lose sight of our medium and long term goals, especially when a project is in crunch time. Setting measurable goals on a rolling cadence helps to combat this by creating a sense of urgency around making steady, incremental progress. Goal setting is also a tool for your manager. The outcomes from tracking these goals can be used as a resource for your manager when performing the agency-wide review process and when making the case to leadership for promotions.

## What's in a Goal?

A "goal" can take many forms depending on the context but, for the scope of our goal setting process, it's worth being explicit about what makes a goal "good" or "bad". We will use a common framework known as SMART Goals. If you browse around for descriptions of this framework, you will see variations in what each letter stands for but, for our purposes, SMART stands for:

### S - Specific

Your goals should be specific enough that, at any given time, it is clear if that goal is "done" or "not done". Ideally if it is not done, you should be able to say "this goal is two-thirds of the way done" or "I'm 20% done with this goal". That level of incrementalism isn't always possible, but it's a good thing to shoot for. To make your goals specific, try to distill the _intent_ of what you want to do into specific tasks that would further that intention. The goal statement "In the next 6 months, I will contribute to the teams communication with the public" is unspecific. "In the next 6 months, I will publish 2 blog posts to our team blog" is specific.

### M - Measurable

Your goals should be measurable. Try to define goals that have numeric metrics associated with them. If you define these metrics and pay attention to them, they will create a sense of urgency around making steady, consistent progress over the cycle. This helps avoid the common situation of realizing you have 6 weeks left before your 6 month review but you definitely aren't 75% of the way towards completing your goals. When thinking about these metrics, try to avoid situations where you would find yourself doing things _just for the sake of meeting your goals_. For example, having a goal that involves contributing to pull request reviews is great, but if your metric is comments on PRs, you shouldn't be making superfluous comments just to hit a quota. Consider metrics that are relative to the total work the team will do, such as "I will participate in [x]% of [y] that the team works on in the next 6 months."

Sometimes having goals that are "all or nothing" is unavoidable and that's ok in some cases - just make sure you still have a measurable thing you can point to that defines the goal as "all" or "nothing".

### A - Attainable/Achievable

When you're writing your goal statements, take time to honestly think about where you are in your own professional development journey and the context you're working in. Setting goals that challenge you to solve new, hard technical issues is great but be realistic. If you set a goal that is too much of a leap, you likely end up frustrated with yourself or burnt out.

You should also consider how much "agency" you have as an individual and how much we have as a team. Ask yourself if your goals could be made impossible based on decisions that you or the team do not control. That's not to say you should only define goals that you have complete autonomy over, but it's something to consider. External factors will always be taken into account when it comes time to review your progress. If you have a goal in mind but are unsure of how realistic it is, it never hurts to bring it to the first meeting with your manager anyway.

### R - Relevant

This one is twofold - first, the goal should be relevant to you and your professional journey, as you see it. While your goals must be specific and measurable, your long term vision for your career does not have to be and that vision can inform your goals. For instance, if you are not formally in a leadership position yet but would like to move towards a management track career, think about what leadership-related things you can do now. Maybe you can have goals around mentorship or goals that have you chipping in with project planning responsibilities. Also consider if your goals are relevant to your "hard" skills. Maybe you're strong on the front end but want to define goals relating to backend work to become more well rounded. Maybe you're already known as a strong back end developer and you like it that way so you come with goals that will further cement your reputation as an authority in that area.

Your goals should also be relevant to the team and to the overall goals of the agency. Think about the projects planned for us over the next 6 months and ask yourself if you see opportunities to achieve your goals, within reason. If you feel like you don't know what work is coming up in the next 1-6 months, your manager should be able to share details. If you're unsure if your goals align with our work pipeline, suggest them anyway, as these goals may affect the work plan itself.

### T - Time-bound

Given the 6 or 12 month cadence of our goal setting, this one is somewhat self-explanatory but it's very important to focus on consistent progress. Think about how you can break down your goals into smaller steps. **You're responsible for tracking the metrics associated with your goals**. Think about how far you would like to be after one month, three months, etc. and use that to create helpful time boxes for yourself.

## Timeline

You can choose to do their goal setting on a 6 month or 12 month cycle. 12 month cycles start in January and 6 month cycles will start in January and July. Here is a basic timeline of how a cycle plays out:

1. Your manager will send you a calendar invite formally kicking off a new cycle. In this invite, they will share a roadmap of upcoming projects and priorities. This is meant to help facilitate your goal brainstorming as you try to formulate goals that feel reasonable to complete given our upcoming work, but please don't hesitate to share goals that might not seem to fit in our roadmap. They will also ask you to respond with a few "rough draft" of goal statements, along with any thoughts on them that they want to share, **two full work days before this first meeting.**. This lead time is meant to give your manager time to read them and prepare feedback. In this first meeting, you and your manager discuss the goals and your manager provides feedback. Your manager will likely ask questions like "_why_ did you pick this goal" to better understand the underlying intention, so be prepared to answer those questions.
2. About one week after this first meeting, you and your manager will meet again to review the refined goals. The week between these meetings is time for you to consider feedback and refine your goals. Ideally, by the end of this second meeting, you and your manager both feel good about the goals and are ready to move forward.
3. Shortly after this second meeting, your manager will share a document with your goals for both parties to sign. This formally kicks off your time to work towards your goals
4. Halfway through the cycle, your and your manager will hold a brief "check-in" meeting to review progress on your goals. This is also an opportunity to update or tweak goals to better fit realities that are making the original goals difficult or impossible to achieve, such as changes to our roadmap.
5. At the end of the cycle, you and your manager will meet a final time to review and reflect on your progress towards reaching your goals. You will be expected to share metrics that illustrate your progress towards your goals. This could be as simple as pointing to links of the blog posts you've written or records of team meetings you've organized. It could involve gathering metrics from tools we use such as GitHub. If you didn't meet your goals, odds are you got pretty close and it's almost certainly the case that there were unforeseen external factors that played a role. You and your manager will also discuss if you feel that your progress towards these goals furthered the original intent that led you to writing the goal. If you originally wrote a goal about contributing to back end code because you wanted to become a more well rounded engineer, expect your manager to ask you if you _feel_ more well rounded or if maybe that feeling had a different root cause. This conversation should help you refine your broader professional development goals and inform your goals for future cycles.

## Writing your goal statements

You can try to frame your goal statements as "In the next 6/12 months I will..." but that's not a hard and fast rule. As previously stated, you'll be expected to share a handful of goal statements with your manager a few days ahead of your first meeting, but don't worry too much about perfecting them for this initial meeting. Your manager will help you refine them before they are finalized. There's no set number of finalized goals you should have but a rule of thumb is 1-2 for a 6 month cycle and 3-4 for a 12 month cycle.

In addition to technical goals like "I will have commits on at least [x] number of API endpoints", feel free to think outside the box about what other goals could further yourself and the team. If you have ideas for goals that improve the culture of the team in some way that is outside the scope of our day to day work, share them with your manager. Goals that have to do with interacting with other teams in the agency or contributing to the broader civic technology ecosystem (such as writing blog posts, for instance) can also be great. Above all, remember this process is **for you**. It should feel challenging but not overwhelming. It is meant to help you identify where you want to grow, find ways to make progress towards that growth, and celebrate your development.
